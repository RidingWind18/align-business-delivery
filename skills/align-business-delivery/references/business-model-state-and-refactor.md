# Business Model, State, And Refactor

Use this reference when work changes a core business object, record granularity, binding relation, final result source, state model, ownership boundary, or an existing business flow. It preserves business meaning while a design is changed; it does not teach framework migration or general implementation mechanics.

## Audit Before Design

Do not begin with new tables, fields, record granularity, or option comparison. First establish:

- What each affected record, field, relation, and state represents now.
- Which object is authoritative for final business results, snapshots, and traceability.
- Roles, ownership, uniqueness, capacity, conflict checks, operation order, and state-transition constraints.
- Direct and indirect consumers, including data access, interfaces, pages, reports, imports, jobs, permissions, logs, and audits.
- What downstream behavior must remain unchanged and what behavior is explicitly allowed to change.

When facts are incomplete, record the verified old meaning, the missing evidence, and only the decisions that block the next design step. Do not infer old semantics from a partially changed implementation; use an appropriate source baseline and reachable dependency facts.

When the user says the proposal is too fast, too large, or dependency-heavy, stop proposing new tables or a complete target design. Audit the old semantics and reachable dependencies first, then advance only the current item with at most three blocking questions.

## Traceability Before Version Modeling

Before creating a version, snapshot, or history table, state the actual traceability need. If the need is only immutable evidence at a freeze point, prefer the smallest record that preserves the necessary URL or path, hash, source, time, key configuration, or compact snapshot on the owning business record or an equivalent narrow evidence asset.

Use a full version model only when the confirmed need includes multi-version query, rollback, approval, reuse, or side-by-side version management. Do not add version states, maintenance pages, or migration complexity for an uncertain future need.

## State And Workflow Paradox

Stop local patches when actions have an unclear order, an action remains possible after its business precondition has changed, or related operations contradict each other. Reframe the issue as a business workflow.

For each business operation, establish its prerequisites, allowed and forbidden states, actor and ownership checks, time limits, duplicate-operation behavior, postcondition, terminal-state treatment, and rollback path. Existing work based on the old flow must be classified as retained, reworked, or re-reviewed before implementation continues.

Page success alone does not establish a valid runtime flow. Review wrong role, wrong context, wrong state, wrong time window, duplicate action, terminal recovery, ownership, stale cached state, and sensitive-data exposure where relevant.

## Refactor And Compatibility

List the old chain before defining the new one: entries, visible operations, interfaces, data paths, states, permissions, records, dependent functions, and prior artifacts. State what it supports, what is changing, and what cannot break.

Choose compatibility, migration, controlled rollout, or hard cut from actual usage, historical-data value, external dependencies, state continuity, rollback cost, and explicit acceptance. A controlled rollout is a separate decision: define its scope, default, rollback behavior, and removal condition. Do not preserve or discard old artifacts merely because they are old or were generated by an assistant.

Specify the rollback point for the chosen approach, such as a data boundary, route, switch, configuration, or deployment step. Old entries and data must be kept, redirected, hidden, migrated, archived, or intentionally excluded by an explicit decision.

## Semantic Preservation Batches

Keep these batches distinct:

- Compatibility adaptation.
- Pure structural move.
- Cleanup with evidence.
- Approved business behavior change.
- Persistence-semantic change.

A technical batch must not carry unconfirmed changes to business behavior, states, identifiers, transactions, storage meaning, or runtime workflow. For meaningful replacements, state the old behavior, new behavior, equivalence or approved difference, baseline checked, reachable consumers, and residual risk. Report completion of the current batch separately from completion of the larger target architecture.
